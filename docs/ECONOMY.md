# OTHERREACH — Economy, Markets & Contracts

**Status:** Strong working direction  
**M2 impact:** None

## 1. Goal

The economy should feel like people actually produce, move, consume and request goods without becoming a perfectly closed simulation that can accidentally make the game miserable.

The player participates in the same broad economy as NPC residents, merchants and adventurers.

## 2. Economic actors

Possible actor categories:

- households;
- farms;
- mines;
- workshops;
- merchants;
- caravans;
- settlements;
- factions/governments;
- autonomous adventurers;
- player businesses/properties.

Not every citizen needs full individual accounting.

## 3. Production chains

Prefer comprehensible chains:

**resource → transport → processing → craft → sale → consumption/repair**

Example:

**ore → merchant/caravan → smith → sword → adventurer → repair**

Systemic demand can therefore create quests/contracts naturally.

## 4. Adventurers as consumers/producers

Autonomous adventurers may buy:

- food;
- potions;
- ammunition;
- repair;
- lodging;
- mounts;
- weapons;
- armor;
- components;
- maps.

They may sell:

- loot;
- hides;
- herbs;
- ore;
- salvage;
- crafted goods;
- information.

## 5. Contracts

Systemic needs can create contracts available to player or NPCs:

- material procurement;
- crafting commissions;
- escort;
- delivery;
- bounty;
- investigation;
- repair;
- transport;
- hiring specialist labor.

The contract exists because an actor needs something, not merely because the player entered town.

## 6. Barter

Allow direct trade/barter where culturally appropriate.

An actor can compare:

- money;
- desired materials;
- useful equipment;
- services;
- reputation/relationship.

Avoid making barter mathematically optimal to the point of slowing every transaction.

## 7. Regional pricing

Prices may respond to:

- local production;
- shortages;
- danger;
- transport distance;
- trade-route status;
- faction law/tax;
- current events.

But enforce stability bounds so ordinary necessities do not become unavailable for absurd periods.

## 8. Stabilizers

Potential game-economy safeguards:

- baseline production;
- essential-good reserves;
- capped elasticity;
- regional imports;
- NPC consumption limits;
- emergency faction/government supply;
- restocking floors;
- substitution goods.

The goal is **responsive**, not economically pathological.

## 9. Money sources

Currency can enter circulation through:

- treasury/faction payments;
- bounties;
- treasure;
- resource purchasing;
- trade with external regions;
- minting or established currency institutions.

## 10. Money sinks

Necessary sinks include:

- repairs;
- taxes;
- rent;
- wages;
- food;
- lodging;
- transportation;
- crafting services;
- property;
- building;
- training;
- market fees;
- luxury goods;
- loss/destruction.

Without sinks, autonomous agents accumulate money forever.

## 11. Banks and storage

Banks can provide:

- safe storage;
- account/legacy storage;
- transfer services;
- settlement-to-settlement financial transfer;
- escrow/contracts;
- later multiplayer mail/market support.

Physical goods still require logistics unless a special magical/Other/technical system moves them.

## 12. Markets

Potential forms:

- direct shop stock;
- local market stalls;
- buy/sell orders;
- contracts;
- auctions;
- caravan merchants.

Do not assume a global auction house in single-player.

## 13. Famous makers and provenance

Crafted items may record makers and significant modifications.

Economic value can reflect:

- maker reputation;
- rare components;
- historical ownership;
- quality;
- famous events.

## 14. Design rule

> Simulate enough economy to generate consequences and opportunities; stabilize enough economy to remain a game.
